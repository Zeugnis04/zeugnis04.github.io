A combination of Hawaiʻian food from Yama's Fish Market.<Sidenote>2332 Young St, Honolulu, HI 96826, United States</Sidenote>

- *ahi poke*: One of the most common kinds of poke in Hawaiʻi
- *lomilomi salmon*: Basically a Hawaiʻian tomato and salmon salad. Common accompaniment to poi or kalua pork.
- *laulau*: Laulau itself refers to the tī (kī in Hawaiʻian) leaves that are used to wrap the meat inside (pork in my case). Taro leaves in the inside are very much edible, and they are slow-cooked for a long time for tenderness. It is related to palusami of Samoa, though laulau typically does not contain coconut cream. Similar dishes from Polynesia generally contain coconut milk or cream. [Lūʻau (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C5%AB%CA%BBau_(food)) is an interesting read.
- *poi*: Poi is the traditional staple food of Polynesia. In Hawaiʻi, poi vary in consistency and sometimes classfied by the terms "one-finger", "two-finger", and "three-finger" by the number of fingers needed to scoop it and eat. Another interesting aspect of poi is that it ferments naturally and changes significantly from sweet to sour. The version I had in this picture was more on the sour side, almost resembling plain yogurt.
- *haupia*: Haupia is a traditional coconut pudding of Hawaiʻi (and Polynesia overall). It is traditionally made from coconut cream or milk with starch from taro or breadfruit.

Next are photos from a Micronesian market<Sidenote id="lol">which was named Micronesia Mart. 1745 Kalākaua Ave, Honolulu, HI 96826, United States</Sidenote>. Photos are mostly food products from the store, but it serves various goods from Yap, Chuuk, Pohnpei and Kosrae, from kava and betel nuts to limestone powder<Sidenote id="tmi">which are states/regions of the Federated States of Micronesia</Sidenote>. We bought kon (*kkón*<Sidenote>orthography from Trukese--English Dictionary Pwpwuken Tettenin Fóós: Chuuk-Ingenes (Goodenough and Sugita, 1980) *kkón*: pounded breadfruit pudding (without anything added).</Sidenote>), basically pounded breadfruit for tasting. Owners of the store did inform that the kon might have been quite fermented as it has been cooked some time before. It had an interesting aftertaste to it, somewhat fruity, but not as fermented as we expected. It does quite stick to the hand and has a doughey texture overall.

- *kale māmoe*: Literally lamb curry. Not very different from Japanese instant curry with lamb meat.
- *mulpipi*: Turkey tail. It has been part of Samoan food culture as meat byproducts (Spam and fatty lamb cuts from New Zealand) started showing up sometime after World War II, and turkey tails came shortly thereafter<Sidenote>[Samoans Await The Return Of The Tasty Turkey Tail](https://www.npr.org/sections/thesalt/2013/05/14/182568333/samoans-await-the-return-of-the-tasty-turkey-tail)</Sidenote>. Turkey tail refers to the fatty, gland-filled, triangular-shaped piece of flesh at the end of a turkey's back, not literally the *tail* of turkeys.
- *taro*: Taro, by itself, is the staple food of Polynesia. I had the feeling that it's firmer than potatoes or sweet potatoes. Slightly sweet and has a good sweet scent coming together.
- *palusami*: Counterpart of the Hawaiʻian laulau. It contains coconut cream together. Taro leaves are cooked and has some savoriness that goes very well with other Samoan food served together.
- *sapa sui*: As expected, sapa sui comes from Chinese chop suey. Not very exotic, it's what you would expect from vermicelli noodle-dominated chop suey (like the Korean japchae).
