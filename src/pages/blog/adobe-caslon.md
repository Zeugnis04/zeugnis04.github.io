## A Brief Thank You to Adobe

Adobe Originals is one of my favorite type foundries — not just because of its beautiful fonts, but because of its lasting impact on digital typography. Backed by Adobe's leading technology, the foundry has consistently set high standards for modern digital type. Their font library offers professional-grade typefaces packed with advanced OpenType features. Adobe has also produced some of the most refined revivals of classic typefaces — like Caslon, which we will introduce in this post — making significant contributions to the evolution of digital type design.

What many don't realize is that Adobe's connection to typography runs deep. Type was a key part of their very first product: the PostScript page description language. In the early days of desktop publishing, Adobe digitized classic typefaces from renowned foundries like Berthold, ITC, Monotype, and Linotype to demonstrate the power of digital type. These efforts proved that digital fonts could not only match, but often exceed the quality of traditional metal type. As a result, Adobe's own type foundry, known today as Adobe Originals, is still a solid foundry with the best designers of type in the industry.

Despite recent frustrations with Adobe's pricing changes and customer support, I still like their Adobe Fonts service very much. Included with the standard Creative Cloud subscription, it remains one of the most accessible and expansive cloud-based font libraries available — offering a vast range of high-quality typefaces. Whether you're looking for reliable classics or cutting-edge contemporary designs, Adobe Fonts continues to be a powerful and convenient tool for designers.

## A Brief History of Caslon

Before discussing the modern revival Adobe Caslon Pro, let's explore Caslon — the serif typeface originally cut by William Caslon I. While modern revivals are typically called simply *Caslon*, I'll maintain a distinction here for clarity: when I refer to Caslon in this article, I mean the broader family and its descendants, including Adobe Caslon (Pro).

Caslon is hardly an obscure typeface — it stands as a celebrated classic from the metal-type era. The typeface is famously associated with early printings of the U.S. Declaration of Independence{% marginfigure 'us declaration' '/assets/img/adobe-caslon/USDeclaration.webp' 'U.S. Declaration of Independence'%} and has decorated the pages of magazines like [*The New Yorker*](https://www.newyorker.com/){% marginfigure 'From the New Yorker' '/assets/img/adobe-caslon/thenewyorker.webp' 'From the New Yorker magazine, Shohei Ohtani and the Dodgers Are a Sight to Behold'%}. First released in 1725, Caslon retains an organic structure reminiscent of pen-drawn letterforms, distinguishing it from more geometric modern typefaces. This characteristic places it among the *old-style serifs*, a category that includes other notable faces such as Jenson, Garamond, and Minion. Notably, William Caslon himself, a skilled punchcutter, created the original metal type through the traditional craft of punchcutting.

According to John Nichols{% sidenote 'bibliography' 'Nichols, John (1782). Biographical and Literary Anecdotes of William Bowyer, Printer, F.S.A. and of Many of His Learned Friends,: Containing an Incidental View of the Progress and Advancement of Literature in this Kingdom, from the Beginning of the Present Century to the End of the Year 1777.'%}, the accuracy of his work came to the attention of prominent London printers, who advanced him money to carve steel punches for printing, first for foreign languages and then, as his reputation developed, for the Latin alphabet. British printers then had hard times making their own types and were forced to use foreign equipments, and as a consequence you may find prominent influence from Dutch baroque typefaces of the period.

Caslon was adopted rapidly and became widely used, but its popularity briefly declined in the late 18th century. This decline coincided with the emergence of transitional typefaces like Baskerville, followed soon after by the rise of modern designs influenced by Bodoni. However, interest in 18th-century typography was revived in the 19th century, particularly with the Arts and Crafts movement. This resurgence brought Caslon back into favor, especially in fine book printing and display settings like advertising, with companies such as the Chiswick Press leading the way.

In the United States, Caslon evolved into a broad typographic category rather than a single design. Many American foundries created new versions—often heavily modified and sometimes pirated — that included features like shorter descenders to align with the American common line, as well as variations like bold, condensed, or lining figures. By the 1920s, American Type Founders offered a wide variety of these styles, some labeled only by number. Major hot-metal typesetting manufacturers such as Linotype, Monotype, Intertype, and Ludlow also released their own versions of Caslon for their typesetting machines.

## Caslon as a Baroque Typeface

As a Baroque typeface, Caslon exhibits several distinctive traits typical of the period. One of the most noticeable is the inconsistency in the letterform axis, a trait shared by many Baroque typefaces. Personally, I found this aspect a bit unconventional at first, and it took some time to get used to. If you look at the example below, which shows Adobe Caslon Italic, you'll clearly see this variation in axis across different characters. Another detail worth highlighting is the steep angle of the italics, which can reach up to 20°, giving the typeface a dynamic and expressive quality.

## Adobe Caslon glyphs

Caslon's roman letterforms carry several distinct and easily recognizable features. The uppercase "A" has a subtle concave notch at the top left, while the "G" lacks the typical downward spur on its lower right. The "M" features straight sides, and the "W" is unique with three terminals at its top. The lowercase "b" includes a small, tapered stroke at the bottom left, and the "a" ends in a gentle ball terminal. Overall, both ascenders and descenders are relatively short, and stroke contrast is moderate, especially at body text sizes — contributing to Caslon's readable, balanced appearance.

In the italic version, several characters are given more expressive forms. The "h" curves inward, and the "A" leans sharply, emphasizing its slanted posture. Flourishes and swashes appear on letters like "Q", "T", "v", "w", and "z" in the original design — though not all modern revivals preserve these stylistic touches. Interestingly, the italic "J" includes a crossbar, and Caslon even used a rotated version of this letter in his specimens to represent the British pound symbol. Adobe Caslon also includes alternative glyphs among with the more *modern*-looking glyphs.

{% maincolumn '/assets/img/adobe-caslon/adobecaslon2.webp' 'Adobe Caslon Pro Regular and Italic glyphs'%}

Adobe Caslon also includes a wide range of alternate glyphs, which is a welcome feature for type enthusiasts—especially those interested in more decorative italic styles with swashes, such as the distinctive *J* mentioned earlier. It's also worth comparing these with other symbols, like the Euro sign, to appreciate the attention to stylistic detail. In addition to keeping traditional ligatures, Adobe Caslon introduces new ones, such as Th, which wasn’t part of the original metal typeface.
